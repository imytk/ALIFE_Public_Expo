## Zone 1: Welcome to "Life As It Could Be"

### Welcome

What if life isn't only what we see around us — the plants, animals, microbes, and people — but something far stranger and more varied than biology has yet produced? What if life could be digital, robotic, or built from an entirely different chemistry on a planet we've never visited? And how would we even know it when we found it?

These are the questions at the heart of **Artificial Life** — a field known as ALife — and they are the questions this exhibition is built around. ALife sits at the intersection of biology, computer science, robotics, chemistry, and philosophy. It doesn't just ask what life *is*; it asks what life *could be*.

Here, you won't just read about these ideas. You'll encounter simulations you can interact with, videos of real research, and experiments you can think along with. Some of what you see may challenge what you thought you knew about life. That's the point.

Your journey into *life as it could be* starts here.

---

### What is Artificial Life?

In the mid-1980s, computer scientist [Christopher Langton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christopher_Langton) formally established the field of Artificial Life, defining it as "the study of artificial systems that exhibit behaviors characteristic of natural living systems." The aim was never simply to copy nature — it was to use a *synthetic* approach to discover the universal principles underlying life in any form.

Rather than observing and analysing organisms that already exist, ALife researchers build simplified systems from scratch and watch to see whether life-like properties emerge. This sounds straightforward, but it immediately raises hard questions: which properties are we looking for? What does "life-like" actually mean? How do we decide?

The tools we use range from software and computers to robots to chemistry — and often combinations of all three. We want to know how complex, stable patterns emerge from surprisingly simple rules — a flock of starlings has no leader, yet moves with extraordinary coordination. We want to know whether intelligence, self-organisation, and adaptation can arise without a biological brain, and what the minimum ingredients for thinking or learning actually are. And we want to know what the absolute minimum is for something to be considered alive at all, and whether we'd recognise it in a computer program, a robot, or a test tube.

ALife helps us understand the life we already know, and opens up the possibility space for life we haven't yet encountered — on other planets, in our own designs, or somewhere we haven't imagined yet.

---

### The ALife Approach: Building to Understand

Traditional biology works largely by observation and analysis — studying life as it is. ALife proposes a different method: *build it to understand it*.

Consider a colony of thousands of ants that, with no single leader, collectively find the shortest path to food. The ALife approach is to build one digital ant, give it a few simple rules, release thousands of copies into a simulated environment, and see whether colony-level intelligence emerges on its own. If it does, that tells us something profound about how real ant colonies work. If it doesn't, we learn something too.

This synthetic method does two things that observation alone cannot. First, it tests our theories: if we think we understand how something works, we should be able to build it. If our model fails, our understanding was incomplete. Second, it lets us venture beyond the life we know — by tweaking the rules, we can ask what life might look like under different conditions, different physical laws, or on a different planet. We can simulate thousands of generations of evolution in a few hours; the same process in nature takes tens of thousands of years.

The practical applications are real and growing. ALife-inspired methods have produced [antenna designs for NASA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evolved_antenna) that no human engineer had thought of, informed [agent-based models of epidemic spread](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agent-based_model_of_epidemic_spread) used in public health policy, and given rise to entire genres of [generative art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Generative_art) that grow and evolve according to their own internal rules. But the deeper motivation is always the same — to understand what life is, how it works, and how far it extends.

## Zone 2: Walking the Line Between "Living" and "Non-Living"

### Where Do You Draw The Line?

We tend to feel confident that we know life when we see it. A dog is alive; a rock is not. But push on that intuition even slightly and things get complicated quickly. Is a virus alive? What about fire — it consumes, it grows, it responds to its environment. What about a crystal that slowly builds itself into a perfect geometric structure, or an AI that learns and adapts?

Scientists and philosophers have been wrestling with these questions for centuries, and they still haven't fully settled them. The boundary between living and non-living is less a clear line than a foggy gradient — and the closer you look, the foggier it gets. This zone is an invitation to sit with that uncertainty. As you read through the examples here, ask yourself: what do *you* think life actually requires?

---

### The Characteristics of Life: A Scientific View

To bring some structure to the debate, biologists have converged on a set of properties that most living things share. These aren't a strict definition of life so much as a useful framework — a checklist that, if something checks all the boxes, gives us good reason to call it alive. Miss even one, and things start to get interesting.

The seven characteristics are:

- **Organisation** — Living things are highly structured, built from specialised components (like cells) that work together in coordinated ways. A single cell is already extraordinarily complex; a multicellular organism is complexity stacked on complexity.
- **Metabolism** — Life takes in energy and materials from its environment and transforms them to grow, maintain, and reproduce itself. Whether it's a plant converting sunlight into sugar or an animal digesting a meal, life is, at its core, a continuous chemical process.
- **Homeostasis** — Living systems actively maintain a stable internal environment even as external conditions shift. Your body keeps itself at roughly 37°C whether you're in a heatwave or a blizzard. A fish carefully regulates the salt concentration in its blood even as the ocean's salinity fluctuates around it.
- **Growth** — Living organisms increase in size and complexity in an organised way. A seedling becoming an oak, or a single fertilised cell becoming a human being, is not just an increase in mass — it's a directed, structured unfolding.
- **Reproduction** — Life produces more of itself, passing on information to the next generation. This is the mechanism by which life persists through time, and what makes evolution possible.
- **Response to Stimuli** — Living things react to their environment. A plant bends toward light. A cat flinches at a loud noise. Even a bacterium tumbles away from a toxic chemical.
- **Adaptation** — Over many generations, populations change to better suit their environments. The polar bear's fur, the cactus's water storage, bacteria's resistance to antibiotics — all are the accumulated outcomes of evolution working on variation across time.

---

### When Non-Living Things Fake It

Here's where the checklist starts to earn its keep. Many non-living systems can pull off one or two of these characteristics convincingly — which is exactly what makes them useful for thinking about what life actually *is*.

An assembly line uses a blueprint to produce thousands of identical copies — that looks a lot like reproduction. A salt crystal in solution grows steadily larger as molecules attach to its surface — that looks a lot like growth. A motion-sensor light responds to movement in its environment. An evolutionary algorithm, run over thousands of generations, can "evolve" a program that learns to walk with surprising grace.

None of these are alive. But the question worth sitting with is: *what exactly is missing?* The answer isn't always as obvious as it first seems.

---

### The Fringes That Cause Debate

More interesting still are the cases that aren't obvious imitations — entities that genuinely possess some of life's characteristics, but not all, and which resist easy classification.

[Viruses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virus) contain genetic material and can reproduce and evolve — but only by hijacking the machinery of a host cell. They have no metabolism of their own, no cellular structure. Whether they count as alive depends almost entirely on how you define the word. [Crystals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crystal) grow, self-organise, and can even repair fractures in their structure — but their growth is purely physical accretion, with no genetic information and no reproduction in any meaningful biological sense. Fire consumes fuel, grows, and responds dramatically to changes in its environment, which is why ancient cultures so readily treated it as a living force — but it has no cells, no genetic code, and doesn't produce offspring.

[Prions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prion) are perhaps the strangest case: misfolded proteins that cause other proteins to misfold in the same way, spreading through a system and causing devastating diseases like [CJD](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Creutzfeldt%E2%80%93Jakob_disease) and [scrapie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scrapie). That's a form of replication — but prions are just molecules, with no cells, no metabolism, nothing beyond that single eerie trick.

And then there are artificial systems. As robots become more sophisticated, as AIs learn and adapt, as synthetic biology produces [cells assembled from scratch](https://www.jcvi.org/research/first-self-replicating-synthetic-bacterial-cell) in the lab, the question sharpens: if an artificial system could genuinely adapt, reproduce, and self-organise without biological components, would it be alive? This is no longer purely hypothetical. It is a question ALife researchers are actively working toward.

The definition of life isn't a fixed answer — it's an ongoing negotiation between science, philosophy, and the endlessly surprising world we keep discovering. That negotiation is one of the central projects of Artificial Life.

As you explore this zone, you'll encounter images and videos of different systems and phenomena. For each one, consider which of these characteristics are present, which are absent — and where, if anywhere, you'd draw the line.

## Zone 3: History of Artificial Life

### Ancient Dreams & Mechanical Wonders

The desire to create life from scratch is almost as old as human thought itself. Long before anyone had the word "technology", people were already imagining it: clay figures animated by divine words, bronze giants forged by gods, statues that could move and speak. These weren't just stories — they were expressions of a question that still sits at the heart of ALife today: what would it actually take to make something alive?

When philosophy gave way to engineering, that question became a craft. [Heron of Alexandria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hero_of_Alexandria), working in the 1st century AD, devised steam-powered engines and mechanical birds that could sing and move — remarkable feats of engineering for the ancient world. Fourteen centuries later, [Leonardo da Vinci](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leonardo_da_Vinci) sketched out a [clockwork knight](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leonardo%27s_robot) capable of sitting up, waving its arms, and moving its jaw — designs that were only reconstructed and confirmed to be functional centuries after his death. Most famously, the French inventor [Jacques de Vaucanson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacques_de_Vaucanson) unveiled a life-sized mechanical duck in 1739 that could quack, drink water, and simulate digestion — a sensation that toured Europe and made Vaucanson one of the most celebrated engineers of his age.

None of these automata were truly alive, of course. But they were doing something important: showing that the *behaviours* of living things — movement, response, even the appearance of feeding — could be studied, isolated, and replicated. The question of life was becoming, slowly, an engineering problem.

---

### From Automata to Algorithms

The next leap was more radical. In the mid-20th century, a handful of thinkers began to argue that you didn't need a physical body to study life at all. The real essence of living systems, they suggested, wasn't in their materials — it was in their *logic*.

[Alan Turing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alan_Turing) showed in the 1930s and 40s that any process that could be described precisely enough could, in principle, be computed. This was a strange and powerful idea: it meant that the step-by-step processes of biology — cell division, inheritance, growth — might be expressible as programs. Turing went further, proposing a model of [morphogenesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turing_pattern) — how organisms develop their physical forms — based purely on the interactions of simple chemical signals. It was one of the first serious attempts to explain a biological phenomenon through computation, and it turned out to be [correct](https://www.nature.com/articles/nature11536).

[John von Neumann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_von_Neumann) took a different angle, working out the theoretical design for a machine that could build complete copies of itself — a computational mirror of biological reproduction. His [self-reproducing automata](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Von_Neumann_universal_constructor) remain a foundational concept in ALife. Then in 1970, mathematician [John Conway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Horton_Conway) introduced something deceptively simple: [the Game of Life](https://conwaylife.com/), a grid of cells each either "on" or "off", following three rules about their neighbours. Given the right starting pattern, it could produce structures that moved, replicated, and interacted — none of it planned, all of it emerging from those three rules. It became one of the most-studied programs in history, and it planted a seed: maybe complexity didn't need to be designed. Maybe it just needed a few good rules and enough space to run.

---

### The ALife Field Takes Shape

These ideas had been percolating across mathematics, biology, computer science, and philosophy for decades, but they hadn't yet found a home together. That changed in September 1987, when [Christopher Langton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christopher_Langton) organised the first Workshop on Artificial Life in Los Alamos, New Mexico.

It was an unusual gathering — biologists sat next to computer scientists, physicists next to philosophers — united by a shared conviction that life was something you could study by *building* it, not just observing it. Langton gave this conviction a name: *"life as it could be."* The goal wasn't to simulate the life we already knew, but to discover the universal principles behind life in any form — whether it ran on carbon, silicon, or something no one had thought of yet. Rather than just dissecting organisms, ALife would build them from scratch. Rather than seeking laws that applied only to biology, it would seek laws applicable whether the medium was carbon, silicon, or something entirely alien. And rather than remaining a scattered set of curiosities, it would become a community — with [its own conferences](https://alife.org/), dedicated journals, and research groups worldwide. Many of the people who gathered in Los Alamos that year are still active researchers today — which, in a field obsessed with how things persist and evolve, feels somehow fitting.

The modern era of Artificial Life continues to challenge our deepest assumptions about what it means to be alive. This is just the broad sweep of that history — the interactive timeline in this zone traces the story in more detail, from ancient automata to the latest experiments in artificial chemistry.

## Zone 4: Emergence & Self-Organisation

### What is Emergence?

A [murmuration of starlings](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=V4f_1_r80RY) — thousands of birds wheeling and folding across the sky in perfect unison — looks like it must have a conductor. It doesn't. There is no lead bird, no central brain directing the flock. Each individual bird follows a handful of simple rules about its nearest neighbours: stay close, don't collide, match speed. From those humble instructions, something extraordinary emerges at the level of the whole.

This is **emergence**: the process by which complex, surprising, or intelligent-seeming patterns arise from the simple interactions of many individual components. The flock's apparent intelligence isn't stored anywhere inside it — it's a property that only exists at the level of the collective. No single bird is doing anything remotely as impressive as the group.

In ALife, emergence is central. We design virtual worlds where agents follow simple instructions and then watch what happens — often with results that surprise even the researchers who built the system. It's a reminder that complexity doesn't require a grand plan or a top-down design. It can grow from the bottom up, from rules simple enough to write on a napkin.

---

### Self-Organisation: Order Without a Blueprint

Closely related to emergence is **self-organisation** — the ability of a system to arrange itself into structured, coherent patterns without any external guidance or central command. If emergence describes what these systems produce, self-organisation describes the mechanism by which they produce it: order arising from the rules of the components themselves, not from anything imposed from outside.

A snowflake is a perfect example. Its intricate, six-fold symmetry emerges entirely from the physical properties of water molecules cooling and bonding — there's no template, no designer, no blueprint. The same principle is at work in the [Belousov-Zhabotinsky reaction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Belousov%E2%80%93Zhabotinsky_reaction) — a chemical system that spontaneously produces beautiful, rotating spiral patterns from the local interactions of molecules, with no one telling it to. You can watch it happening in real time in the video for this zone.

For ALife researchers, self-organising systems are both a source of inspiration and a subject of study. By understanding the simple rules that generate order in nature, we can build digital and physical systems that organise, adapt, and behave in complex ways — all from the bottom up. These systems remind us of something remarkable: that the universe has a deep tendency toward order, even in the absence of anyone designing it.

---

### Why Emergence Matters for ALife

Emergence and self-organisation offer something invaluable: a way to produce life-like complexity without having to design every detail. This is enormously powerful, both scientifically and practically.

Instead of programming an intelligent robot behaviour by behaviour, ALife researchers might give thousands of tiny virtual agents a few simple rules for finding food and let the system run. What emerges is often a robust, optimised foraging strategy — a collective intelligence that no single agent possesses. Real-world examples of this approach include [ant-colony optimisation algorithms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ant_colony_optimization_algorithms), which are now used in logistics and network routing, and [swarm robotics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swarm_robotics) systems being developed for [search and rescue](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/scirobotics.abo6628), construction, and environmental monitoring.

Understanding emergence also gives us tools for thinking about biological systems we couldn't otherwise study — how the [brain's global dynamics emerge from billions of neurons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neural_oscillation), how the [immune system coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Immune_network_theory) without a central command, and how ecosystems maintain their resilience in the face of constant perturbation. And it opens up speculative possibilities: forms of life that have no single body or brain, but exist as distributed, self-organising networks of interacting parts.

## Zone 5: Evolution & Adaptation

### What is Evolution?

Life on Earth is astonishingly diverse — from deep-sea bacteria to rainforest canopies to the person standing next to you. That diversity, and the uncanny way organisms fit their environments, is largely the work of evolution: one of the most powerful and counterintuitive processes in nature.

Evolution is not about individuals improving themselves during their lifetimes. It operates on *populations*, across *generations*, and it has no goal or direction. What it does have is a mechanism — and that mechanism is almost absurdly simple. Individuals within a population vary, and some of that variation is heritable. Some variations happen to work better in a given environment, so the individuals that carry them survive and reproduce more successfully, passing those traits on. Repeat this across thousands of generations and you get something extraordinary: the polar bear's insulating blubber, the orchid's precisely shaped flower, the bacterium that shrugs off antibiotics. Not designed, not intended — just the accumulated outcome of differential survival, playing out across time.

---

### The Power of Adaptation

The most visible result of evolution is **adaptation**: features and behaviours that help an organism survive and reproduce in its particular environment. The fit is often extraordinarily precise. The [Arctic fox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arctic_fox) has fur that changes colour with the seasons, white in winter and brown in summer, providing camouflage in both. The [mantis shrimp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mantis_shrimp) has sixteen types of photoreceptor in its eyes — humans have three — giving it a view of the world we can barely imagine. The [tardigrade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tardigrade), a microscopic animal found in moss and leaf litter, can survive the vacuum of space, temperatures near absolute zero, and radiation doses that would kill any other animal on Earth.

None of these are accidents. They are what happens when random variation meets consistent selection pressure, repeated over millions of generations. In ALife, we use this same logic deliberately. We define an artificial environment and a measure of fitness, and then we let simulated evolution run. Often, the solutions that emerge are ones no human designer would have thought of — strange gaits, unexpected body plans, novel problem-solving strategies that are effective precisely because they weren't constrained by human intuition.

---

### Evolutionary Algorithms: Borrowing Nature's Design Process

How do you design something truly innovative when you don't know what it should look like? One answer — increasingly common in engineering, AI, and even art — is to let evolution do the designing. This approach, called [evolutionary computation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evolutionary_computation), applies the logic of natural selection to digital systems.

Instead of DNA, we work with strings of code — digital "genes" that encode the characteristics of virtual creatures, robot controllers, or artistic designs. We create a population, define a fitness function, and simulate the cycle of selection, reproduction, and mutation. Run for hundreds or thousands of generations, this process regularly produces solutions that are surprising, elegant, and effective — and that no human engineer had anticipated.

The results have been striking. A [NASA project using evolutionary algorithms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evolved_antenna) produced antenna designs that outperformed anything a human team had created, with strange, asymmetric shapes that looked almost biological. Researchers at [OpenAI](https://openai.com/research/evolution-strategies) have used evolution-inspired methods to train neural networks. The pharmaceutical industry uses [evolutionary approaches to drug discovery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evolutionary_algorithm#Applications), searching vast chemical spaces for promising compounds. And in the arts, [evolutionary music composition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evolutionary_music) and [genetic visual art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evolutionary_art) have produced works of genuine aesthetic interest. Evolution, it turns out, works just as well in silicon as it does in nature.

## Zone 6: Complexity from Simplicity

### Complicated vs. Complex

We use the word "complex" to mean many things in everyday life — difficult, intricate, hard to understand. But in science, *complexity* has a more specific meaning, and it's importantly different from being merely *complicated*.

A complicated system — a jet engine, a mechanical watch — has many parts, but its behaviour is in principle predictable. Every component has a defined role. If you understand the parts, you can understand the whole. These systems are designed top-down, and they do what they're designed to do.

A **complex system** is different. Its parts interact in ways that are non-linear: small changes in one place can produce disproportionate effects elsewhere. This is the famous [butterfly effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Butterfly_effect) — the idea, from chaos theory, that a butterfly flapping its wings in Brazil might set off a chain of atmospheric effects that eventually produces a storm in Texas. It's a striking image for a real phenomenon: in non-linear systems, causes and effects are not proportional, and small perturbations can cascade unpredictably. Complex systems also exhibit **emergent properties** — behaviours that are not present in any individual component and cannot be predicted by studying the parts in isolation. The whole is genuinely different from the sum of its parts. And they tend to adapt: they change in response to their environment in ways that no one designed.

Think of a rainforest, a human brain, or a city. No blueprint dictates their behaviour. Their functionality and resilience emerge from countless simple, local interactions — and they continue to surprise us.

---

### Feedback Loops and Global Patterns

Complex systems are all around us, shaping things we interact with every day. The key mechanism is the **feedback loop** — where the output of a process feeds back in to change that same process.

In a financial market, a single trader's decision to sell can cause a small dip in price. That dip triggers others to sell, which drops the price further, which triggers more selling. A small local action cascades into a global event: a market crash. The [2010 Flash Crash](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2010_flash_crash), in which the US stock market lost nearly a trillion dollars in value in minutes before partially recovering, is a vivid recent example — caused by a cascade of automated trading decisions, each following simple local rules, producing a global outcome no individual trader intended or controlled.

Ecosystems work the same way. More prey means more food for predators, so the predator population grows. As it grows, it puts pressure on the prey population, which shrinks — which then reduces the food available to predators, causing their numbers to fall too. This push-and-pull creates a dynamic, self-regulating balance. The [lynx and snowshoe hare populations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lotka%E2%80%93Volterra_equations) of northern Canada oscillate in a ten-year cycle that has been tracked for over a century — a textbook example of complex feedback producing orderly, predictable patterns at the population level from the chaotic individual level below.

---

### Modelling Complex Systems

Because complex systems are too intricate to fully grasp through observation alone, we use mathematical and computational models to study them. By encoding simple rules of interaction and running simulations, we can watch complexity emerge and ask questions that would otherwise be impossible to answer.

Some of the most important questions in science are questions about complex systems. How do simple molecules, following basic rules of attraction and repulsion, spontaneously organise themselves into the proteins and membranes that make cells possible? [Agent-based models of epidemic spread](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agent-based_model_of_epidemic_spread) were used during the COVID-19 pandemic to test the likely effects of different public health interventions. [Climate models](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Climate_model) attempt to capture the non-linear interactions between atmosphere, ocean, ice, and land that determine how the planet will respond to rising CO₂.

ALife extends this further — not just modelling life as it is, but exploring what life *could* be. Could life emerge from different chemical building blocks — silicon rather than carbon, perhaps, or something stranger? Can we build systems where novelty and complexity increase indefinitely, without a pre-set goal — a form of [open-ended evolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open-ended_evolution) that never runs out of room to surprise us? What minimal rules are needed for a group of simple agents to form a functioning society? Can we design self-sustaining digital worlds — [artificial ecosystems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artificial_life#Digital_organisms) — that evolve over time, giving us a living laboratory for understanding the principles of life?

Ultimately, the answers to these questions are highly complex — but the key to finding them begins with a few simple rules.

## Zone 7: The Three Faces of ALife — Soft, Hard, and Wet

ALife is not a single method but a collection of approaches, each exploring life-like properties through a different medium. Researchers have come to call them **Soft**, **Hard**, and **Wet** ALife — corresponding roughly to computation, robotics, and chemistry. Each has its own character, its own kinds of questions, and its own way of getting at what life fundamentally is.

---

### 7A: Hard ALife — Life in Motion

**Hard ALife** explores how life-like properties can emerge in physical, embodied systems — particularly robots. The central claim of this field is that intelligence is not just a property of software; it is shaped by the body that carries it, and by that body's ongoing interaction with the physical world.

This idea — called [embodied intelligence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Embodied_cognition) — runs counter to the classical view of AI, which treats the mind as something that happens in a processor, with the body serving merely as an output device. Hard ALife researchers argue instead that a well-designed physical form can handle problems that would otherwise require complex computation. A flexible limb that bends naturally around an obstacle "solves" a problem that a rigid limb would need an algorithm for. The body, in this sense, computes.

In practice, this has produced some of the most striking robots of recent decades. [Swarm robotics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swarm_robotics) systems — inspired by ant colonies and bird flocks — send large numbers of simple robots to cooperate on tasks no individual robot could manage: [mapping disaster zones](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/scirobotics.abo6628), assembling structures, or monitoring large areas. [Bio-inspired robots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biomimetics) mimic the locomotion of animals — the [cheetah robot](https://www.csail.mit.edu/research/cheetah-robot) from MIT runs and jumps, the [ACM-R5H](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8jTgROhSAaA) swims like a snake, and Harvard's [RoboBee](https://www.seas.harvard.edu/news/2013/05/harvard-creates-first-bee-sized-robot-that-can-fly-and-swim) takes inspiration from insect flight. [Soft robots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soft_robotics) — built from silicone, fabric, or other compliant materials — can squeeze through tight spaces, handle delicate objects, and interact safely with humans in ways rigid machines cannot, and their physical compliance often gives rise to emergent behaviours that weren't explicitly programmed. And [modular robots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modular_self-reconfiguring_robotic_systems), made of interchangeable units that can reconnect and reorganise themselves, exhibit a mechanical form of regeneration — adapting to new tasks or repairing damage by literally rebuilding themselves.

### 7B: Soft ALife — Life in Lines of Code

**Soft ALife** is where life-like systems are built entirely in computation. There are no physical bodies, no wet chemistry — just data structures, algorithms, and the interactions between them. Think of it as building new universes inside a computer's memory, then watching what evolves.

This branch of ALife allows us to work at speeds impossible in the physical world. We can run thousands of generations of evolution in minutes, observe the emergence of complex behaviours from simple rules, and test theories that would take centuries — or longer — to play out in nature.

The range of what Soft ALife encompasses is broad. [Agent-based models](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agent-based_model) populate digital environments with virtual agents — abstract organisms, simulated people, economic actors — each following simple rules, whose interactions produce emergent behaviours that help us study disease spread, ecological dynamics, and social trends. [NetLogo](https://ccl.northwestern.edu/netlogo/), one of the most widely used platforms for this kind of modelling, has been used in everything from studying [wolf-sheep predation dynamics](https://ccl.northwestern.edu/netlogo/models/WolfSheepPredation) to [traffic flow](https://ccl.northwestern.edu/netlogo/models/TrafficBasic) to [the spread of forest fires](https://ccl.northwestern.edu/netlogo/models/FireSimple). Cellular automata — grids of cells following local rules — range from [Conway's Game of Life](https://conwaylife.com/) to more recent systems like [Lenia](https://chakazul.github.io/lenia.html), which push into continuous, fluid, strikingly biological-looking territory. And generative art systems — from [Karl Sims's evolved virtual creatures](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=JBgG_VSP7f8) in the 1990s to contemporary [neural evolution of artistic imagery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Generative_art) — produce outputs that grow, adapt, and surprise even their creators.

The power of Soft ALife lies in abstraction: by stripping life down to its computational logic, we can test fundamental theories about emergence, self-organisation, and evolution without the confounding complexity of biology. The question "what are the minimal rules required for life-like behaviour?" is much easier to ask — and answer — in a simulation.

> 🎮 **Try it yourself:** [Avida-ED](https://avida-ed.msu.edu/) — a digital evolution platform used in real research and university teaching.

> 🎬 **Watch:** [Soft ALife — video](https://youtu.be/8oy353aIE3M)

### 7C: Wet ALife — Life in the Lab

**Wet ALife** takes the question of life back to its most fundamental level: chemistry. Rather than simulating life or building robots that move like animals, Wet ALife researchers work in actual laboratories, using non-biological, pre-biological, or re-engineered biological materials to explore how life-like properties can arise from chemistry itself.

This is, in many ways, the most audacious branch of the field. It doesn't produce models or abstractions — it produces physical things, in test tubes and microfluidic chips, that may genuinely be early-stage life, or at least its closest artificial approximation.

The [Belousov-Zhabotinsky reaction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Belousov%E2%80%93Zhabotinsky_reaction) is a striking entry point: a chemical system that spontaneously generates beautiful, rotating spiral patterns from the interactions of molecules in solution, with no external instruction. It's a natural example of self-organisation in chemistry, and it gives a sense of the kinds of phenomena Wet ALife researchers work to understand and recreate deliberately. [Protocells](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protocell) — simple, self-assembling chemical compartments, essentially tiny bubbles that can encapsulate other chemicals, grow, and divide — are seen as plausible models for the earliest forms of life on Earth. The [Sutherland group at Cambridge](https://www.mrc-lmb.cam.ac.uk/sutherland/) has done influential work on how the chemical building blocks of life might have assembled spontaneously under early-Earth conditions. And [synthetic biology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Synthetic_biology) — an adjacent field that uses engineering principles to redesign biological components — has produced bacteria that [produce insulin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biosynthetic_human_insulin), [detect environmental toxins](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-019-09875-9), and [store data in DNA](https://www.nature.com/articles/nature17946).

By demonstrating how life-like properties can arise spontaneously from non-living chemistry, Wet ALife offers our best current window into how life on Earth might have first begun — and into what life elsewhere in the universe might look like if it evolved from different chemical building blocks. It also raises some of the sharpest ethical questions in the field: as we approach the ability to synthesise genuinely life-like systems from scratch, what responsibilities does that create?

## Zone 8: Artificial Life and Society

### Our Role in Shaping the Future

We live in what scientists now call the [Anthropocene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anthropocene) — an era in which human activity has become a geological force, reshaping the planet's systems on a timescale that has no precedent. Into this already complicated picture, ALife introduces new kinds of systems: ones that can learn, adapt, and in some cases make decisions autonomously. Our power grows. So do our responsibilities.

Many practitioners within the ALife community have recognised this openly. Questions about safety, fairness, and accountability are not peripheral to the field — they are part of it. How do we guide systems built on emergence and self-organisation that can behave in ways their creators didn't anticipate? As we build systems with genuine autonomy, who is accountable when they act in ways that cause harm? Who gets to develop these technologies, and who benefits from them? ALife's insights can help us design solutions that consider equity from the start — but only if that's a deliberate choice. And if we can create new forms of "life" in code, silicon, or a test tube, what does that mean for our own place in the world? How do we define and treat these new entities, and how will living alongside them change us?

These aren't abstract questions. They're already live debates in policy, law, and ethics communities, and the pace of development is accelerating.

---

### Can ALife Help Us Solve Our Biggest Problems?

Beyond the philosophical questions, ALife is already contributing practical tools for tackling what researchers call "wicked problems" — challenges like climate change, poverty, and social inequality that are so entangled with each other that simple, top-down interventions routinely fail or backfire.

The concepts you've encountered in this exhibition — emergence, complexity, self-organisation — are not just intellectually interesting. They're also clues to how these problems work, and how they might be approached differently. [Agent-based models](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agent-based_model) of urban systems are helping city planners understand how traffic, housing, and inequality interact at the level of individual decisions — tools like [UrbanSim](https://www.urbansim.com/) are being used to simulate how policy changes ripple through cities before they're implemented. Evolutionary algorithms are being applied to the design of [more efficient solar panels](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-023-06734-w) and [wind farm layouts](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0960148121017122). [Ecological network models](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecological_network) help conservationists understand which species and habitats are most critical to maintaining ecosystem resilience, informing decisions about where to focus limited resources. And ALife-inspired approaches to [collective intelligence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Collective_intelligence) are being explored as tools for community decision-making and participatory governance — understanding how groups can coordinate and sustain change from the bottom up, without a centralised authority doing the directing.

ALife's deepest lesson here may be this: that the most resilient, adaptive systems — whether biological, social, or technological — are rarely the ones with the most centralised control. They're the ones where intelligence is distributed, where local interactions produce global order, and where the system can reorganise itself when conditions change.

---

### ALife, You, and Us

Artificial Life is not just a scientific field — it's a lens for imagining the future. As biological and artificial intelligence continue to develop in parallel, as hybrid systems blur the line between living and built, and as autonomous agents take on increasingly significant roles in society, the questions ALife has always asked become questions for everyone.

These developments are too important to be decided by scientists and engineers alone. The conversations that will shape what kind of future we build need to include as many voices as possible — including yours. How do we want to live alongside new forms of intelligence? What do we owe to entities we create? What kind of planet, and what kind of civilisation, do we want to hand on?

The answers won't come from a single voice. They'll emerge — as the most important things often do — from all of us together.

## Zone 9: The Future of Artificial Life

### The Future of (A)Life

From ancient myths to the cutting edge of science, the desire to create life is a constant thread in human history. The field of ALife suggests this dream is a matter of *when*, not *if*. But what might the future actually look like when we have genuinely succeeded in creating new forms of life?

One possibility is that we become not just designers, but **co-evolvers** — participants in a process rather than controllers of it. Our social, technological, and even biological systems may begin to evolve in tandem with our artificial creations, the lines between the natural and the artificial dissolving gradually until the distinction no longer holds. Already, [xenobots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xenobot) — tiny living machines assembled from frog cells — can move, carry payloads, and even [self-replicate in ways their designers didn't anticipate](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.2112672118). Artificial biospheres may develop in ways that surprise their creators, generating forms of life that are entirely novel and, by any honest measure, alien.

The deepest legacy of ALife may be to force us to expand our definition of life beyond the biological — to recognise that the processes of life (metabolism, reproduction, adaptation, self-organisation) are substrate-independent, and that life may be an inherent property of complexity itself, not a special privilege of carbon-based chemistry on one small planet.

---

### New Kinds of Intelligence and Existence

ALife invites us to think seriously about modes of existence that have no precedent. What would it mean to communicate with a collective of robots sharing a single distributed consciousness — not a network of independent machines, but something closer to a hive mind? How would a self-organising chemical system "think" or "learn"? These are no longer purely hypothetical questions; [research into distributed cognition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distributed_cognition) and [swarm intelligence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swarm_intelligence) is actively exploring them.

In the arts, ALife principles are already producing work of genuine cultural interest. [Refik Anadol](https://refikanadol.com/) uses machine learning and generative algorithms to create large-scale installations in which data becomes something almost alive — breathing, flowing, self-organising in response to its environment. The musician [Holly Herndon](https://www.hollyherndon.com/) has built AI collaborators that evolve and respond as creative partners. These are early signs of a future in which art may be less a made object and more a living process.

Looking further ahead: if novel artificial life forms interact with biological ecosystems, the planet's ecology could be transformed in ways we cannot fully predict. The line between infrastructure and organism may blur in ways we are only beginning to imagine. What happens to our sense of "nature" when some of its most prominent inhabitants were designed?

---

### The Ethical Frontier

As we look toward this future, we encounter ethical questions unlike any we have faced before. The familiar frameworks — legal responsibility, moral status, the definition of harm — were built for a world in which "life" and "intelligence" were clearly biological. They are not well equipped for what's coming.

Is consciousness a property of biological material, or an emergent property of sufficiently complex information processing? Philosophers like [David Chalmers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Chalmers) have argued that we have no principled reason to assume it requires biology — and if that's right, then an artificial system that demonstrated genuine sentience or the capacity for suffering would raise immediate moral questions about what we owe it. As ALife systems become capable of consequential independent decisions, who is accountable when things go wrong? The creator? The operator? The system itself? Our current legal frameworks, still largely based on the assumption that only humans and certain animals have morally relevant interests, have no good answer. And [synthetic biology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Synthetic_biology) is already producing hybrid systems — [xenobots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xenobot) grown from biological tissue, organisms with [fully synthetic genomes](https://www.jcvi.org/research/first-self-replicating-synthetic-bacterial-cell) — that resist easy classification. How we define and treat these new beings will have implications for ecosystems, for medicine, and for our sense of what it means to be a biological organism at all.

This future promises more than new technologies. It promises — or threatens, depending on your perspective — a fundamental revision of how we understand our place in the universe.

---

### A Closing Thought

The questions explored in this exhibition don't have simple answers. They won't be resolved in a laboratory, or by any single discipline. They'll be answered, slowly and imperfectly, through the conversations, experiments, disagreements, and collective decisions of a global community — including you.

As you leave this exhibition, we invite you to carry these questions with you. Talk about them. Disagree about them. Change your mind. The future of life — artificial and otherwise — will be shaped by what we all decide it should look like.

*Life as it is. And life as it could be.*
