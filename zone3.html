Ancient Dreams & Mechanical Wonders

The desire to create artificial life is almost as old as humanity itself. For millennia, myths, legends, and philosophical inquiries have investigated the possibility of bringing inanimate matter to life. From the clay Golems of ancient folklore to the animated statues of Greek mythology, humans have dreamed of crafting beings that can move, react, and even "think".

Long before the advent of computers, this fascination took shape in mechanical wonders — intricate clockwork figures designed to mimic life with astonishing fidelity, built by skilled artisans and engineers. Some examples you might already be familiar with include:

  • Heron of Alexandria (1st Century AD) — Devised steam-powered engines and mechanical birds that sang and moved.
  • Leonardo da Vinci (c. 1495) — Designed a mechanical knight that could sit up, wave its arms, and open its visor.
  • Jacques de Vaucanson (18th Century) — Built incredibly complex, life-sized automatons, including a mechanical duck that could quack, drink water, and even "digest" and "excrete" food.

These mechanical marvels weren't truly "alive", but they showcased an early curiosity about the mechanisms of life and especially the possibility of replicating them. They laid the conceptual groundwork for a science — and a community — that would emerge centuries later.

From Automata to Algorithms

The true leap towards modern Artificial Life came not just from mechanical ingenuity, but from the realisation that the logic of life could be separated from its physical manifestation. The mid-20th century saw groundbreaking ideas that shifted the focus from building physical machines to understanding the underlying computational and informational processes of life.

  • Alan Turing (1930s–1950s) — His theoretical "Turing Machine" established the concept of universal computation, proving that any computable process could be broken down into simple, logical steps — the blueprint for thinking about biological processes computationally.
  • John von Neumann (1940s–1950s) — Proposed the concept of self-reproducing automata, theorising how a complex machine could be designed to build copies of itself — laying the theoretical foundation for understanding biological reproduction computationally.
  • Early Cellular Automata — Systems like John Conway's Game of Life (1970) showed that astonishingly complex, "life-like" behaviours — including self-organisation and reproduction — could emerge from incredibly simple rules applied to a grid of cells.

These pioneers began to sketch out a radical new idea: that life might be understood not just by its material composition, but by the abstract computational processes that define it.

The ALife Field Takes Shape

The field of Artificial Life as a distinct scientific discipline was formally born in September 1987, with the first international workshop held in Los Alamos, New Mexico. Organised by visionary researcher Christopher Langton, this pivotal event brought together scientists from diverse fields: computer science, biology, physics, mathematics, and philosophy.

Langton famously articulated the core philosophy of this new field: "Life as it could be." The goal was not just to simulate known life, but to synthesise — to build — artificial systems that exhibit the fundamental properties of life like metabolism, self-organisation, adaptation, and reproduction, regardless of their underlying material.

This marked a revolutionary shift in how we think about life:

  • From Analysis to Synthesis — Instead of just dissecting biological organisms, ALife proposes to understand life by creating it from scratch.
  • From Biology to Universal Principles — Seeking to uncover the universal laws of life, applicable whether the medium is carbon, silicon, or something entirely alien.
  • From Obscurity to a Thriving Community — Since 1987, the ALife community has grown exponentially, with regular conferences, dedicated journals, and research groups worldwide. Many researchers from those early ALife conferences are still active in this community today — including at ALIFE 2025 in Kyoto!
