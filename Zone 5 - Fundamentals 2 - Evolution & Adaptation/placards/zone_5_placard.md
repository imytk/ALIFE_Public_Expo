# What is Evolution?

Look around you – from the smallest bacteria to the tallest redwood, life on Earth is incredibly diverse. This astonishing variety, and the diverse ways organisms fit into their environments, is largely thanks to evolution.

At its core, evolution is a powerful, undirected engine of change that operates over countless generations. It's not about individuals "improving" themselves during their lifetime, but about populations changing over time. The key ingredients are:

- **Variation**:  Individuals within a population are never identical, and each one has unique traits encoded in their genes.

- **Inheritance**: These variations can be passed down from parents to offspring.

- **Selection**: Some variations prove more advantageous in a particular environment, allowing those individuals with that variation to survive and reproduce more successfully.

Over vast periods of time (thousands, hundreds of thousands, or even millions of years), this simple cycle of variation, inheritance, and selection sculpts life into ever more complex, efficient, and sometimes bizarre forms. The most visible result of this process is the ability of life to continue to adapt to its surroundings. In Artificial Life, we've found ways to bring this powerful engine into our digital and physical worlds.

# The Power of Adaptation

The main outcome of evolution is "adaptation". An adaptation is a feature (like the colors of a bird's feathers) or behavior (such as the ability to walk on land) that helps an organism survive and reproduce more effectively in its specific environment. It's how life "fits in" perfectly with its surroundings.

Consider a polar bear's thick fur and blubber to stay warm in the Arctic, or a desert fox's large ears to dissipate heat. These are not random features; they are traits that evolved because they provided a survival advantage in particular conditions. The organisms best adapted to their environment are the ones most likely to pass on their genes and ensure the continuation of their species.

In Artificial Life, we leverage this same principle. By setting up "environments" and "fitness goals" for our artificial creatures or programs, we can observe them "adapt" and "evolve" increasingly sophisticated solutions to challenges. We might ask a virtual creature to learn to walk, or a piece of code to solve a complex problem, and then let simulated evolution guide that creature's development. This process often yields solutions that human designers might never have conceived!

# Simulating Evolution through "Artificial" Evolution

How do you design something truly innovative without knowing exactly what it should look like? In Artificial Life, we've borrowed nature's most powerful designer: evolution. This approach is called Artificial Evolution, or, more often, Evolutionary Algorithms.

Instead of working with DNA, we work with digital "genes" — strings of code that define the characteristics of our virtual creatures, robots, or even artistic designs. We create a "population" of these digital individuals, then set up a "fitness function" — a measure of how "good" each individual is at solving a problem (like how fast a virtual robot walks, or how aesthetically pleasing an image is).

Next, we simulate the evolutionary cycle:

- **Selection**: The "fittest" individuals are chosen to be the next generation of parents.

- **Reproduction & Mutation**: The “genes” of the fittest are combined and slightly altered (mutated) to create a new generation of "offspring."

Over hundreds or thousands of generations, this can result in surprisingly complex and optimized solutions for a particular problem. This approach lets us explore vast design spaces, discover novel engineering solutions, and even create art that will continually surprise us, all by letting a process akin to natural selection do the "designing."  It's a testament to the universal power of evolution, a force that works just as effectively in silicon as it does in nature.
